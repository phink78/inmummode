It's 5:40 and everything is going wrong

Why the last three hours of the day break earlier than they feel — and a seven-night way to find where yours does.

It's 5:40 and everything is going wrong. Dinner isn't on. Somebody needs a bath, somebody needs you, and the kitchen has the particular noise of four things happening at once.

This is the hour everyone tries to fix. Better recipes, a stricter bath time, a louder voice. It rarely works, because the evening almost never breaks where it feels loudest. It usually breaks about twenty minutes earlier, somewhere quiet, when a decision got postponed.

Often it goes back further than that. Most people fix the wrong hour. They go after six o'clock. The evening goes wrong at eight in the morning.

Watch before you fix

So the first move is not a fix. For two nights you change nothing at all. You only watch.

Between the end of the working day and the moment you sit down, write down what actually happened, in order. Not what you meant to happen. Rough times are fine:

  1. 17:10 got in
  2. 17:25 started dinner, stopped twice
  3. 17:50 the shouting
  4. 18:30 bath
  5. 19:40 sat down

Then two lines. The worst twenty minutes, and the twenty minutes before them. That pair of lines is the whole method, and it takes about four minutes.

Two nights is enough to see whether the pinch point stays put or moves. Most people find it stays put — and that it isn't where they thought.

One decision, moved to breakfast

Once you can see the pinch point, look at what was being decided inside it. Dinner. Bath or no bath. Whether you're going out.

Then make that decision in the morning instead. Three lines on the fridge at eight: what they eat, where you'll be, what you're skipping. You're not doing anything differently at 5:40. You're arriving at it having already decided.

6pm is not a problem to solve. It's a plan you forgot to make.

Written to you, not to the role

Almost everything else in your week is addressed to the mother of someone — the nursery, the group chat, the apps, often your own family. This isn't. The evening in question is yours. Your child shows up here the way weather does: a condition to plan around, not the subject.

That's also why none of this asks anyone else to change first. Every step is something you can do on your own, in about four minutes an evening.

The seven nights

The free guide takes this further, one small change a night:

  • Nights one and two: watch, and write the sequence down.
  • Night three: move one decision to the morning.
  • Night four: remove one thing entirely. Subtraction, not substitution.
  • Night five: five sentences for the handover, when there's another adult and it keeps turning into a negotiation.
  • Night six: a twenty-minute reset for the evenings that have already gone.
  • Night seven: read the week back and keep the one change that earned its place.

By the end you have one change that has survived a week. That's worth more than four that survive a night.
